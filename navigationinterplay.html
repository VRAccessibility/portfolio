Locomotion and Sensory Cue Interplay in Non-Visual VR Navigation

Evaluating continuous and discrete locomotion paired with uni- and multimodal feedback for BLV users

Virtual reality (VR) usually requires seeing where you are going, making it largely inaccessible to blind and low vision (BLV) users. In this study, I tested how 18 BLV participants navigated virtual obstacle courses using three common VR movement styles (using a joystick, swinging arms, or teleporting) paired with different types of non-visual feedback (3D audio, a vibrating vest, or both). The results showed that success depends heavily on matching the right movement style with the right feedback. For example, continuous movements like walking with a joystick worked best with natural-sounding audio, while the sudden jumps of teleportation were easier to manage with vibration cues. Over time, participants used combinations of both sound and vibration as a fail-safe, allowing them to confidently move faster, even if they wandered slightly from the perfect path.

Project Overview

A mixed-methods VR navigation user study with 18 BLV participants evaluating the effectiveness of the three most commercially ubiquitous non-fatiguing locomotion techniques paired with non-visual directional cues.

Problem

Despite VR adopting continuous and discrete artificial locomotion techniques, understanding of how these mechanics interact with non-visual auditory and vibrotactile guidance remains limited. BLV users face a failure point barrier when attempting to access mainstream VR experiences due to an over-reliance on visual paradigms and a lack of properly aligned non-visual navigation tools.

Key Findings

While participants achieved a high overall success rate (88.07%), efficient navigation heavily depends on the mechanical alignment between movement techniques and sensory feedback. A significant cognitive and mechanical clash emerged: continuous movement techniques benefited from auditory feedback, whereas the discrete jumps of teleportation relied on vibration-only feedback to prevent inefficient wandering.

Problem Definition & Setup

This chapter builds upon foundational pointing tasks to evaluate fully independent non-visual wayfinding. The study employed a 3x3 within-subjects factorial design with 18 BLV participants, crossing three locomotion techniques (sliding joystick, arm-swinging, and teleportation) with three feedback modalities (audio-only, vibration-only, and combined).

Testing utilised the Meta Quest Pro and the bHaptics Tactsuit x40 vest, focusing on commercially available off-the-shelf hardware rather than bespoke laboratory prototypes. The environment was rendered completely black to isolate the effects of the non-visual modalities.

To guide users non-visually, target proximity was conveyed using a looped spatialised piano sound or intensity-scaled vest vibration. For veering guidance, we implemented an attractive, off-course model with a 20-degree central deadzone. If the participant's tracking vector deviated beyond this threshold, spatialised audio (wind sounds) or vibrotactile feedback (on the back motors of the vest) was presented to "pull" them back to the correct heading.

We asked:

  1. How do continuous and discrete locomotion techniques paired with uni- or multimodal non-visual feedback influence objective-based navigation performance?
  2. How do these combinations affect subjective workload, spatial presence, and overall user experience?

Participants navigated through three distinct layouts consisting of straight paths, waypoints, and obstacles, requiring real-time course correction and spatial updating entirely without sight.

Quantitative Results

Performance metrics revealed critical insights into the interplay of movement and feedback:

  • High Success: 428 out of 486 targets were reached within the time limits (88.07% success rate), proving independent non-visual navigation is highly viable.
  • Efficiency Clash: Path Efficiency Ratio (PER) suffered significantly when techniques and cues were misaligned. Joystick efficiency dropped severely with vibration-only feedback, while teleportation was highly inefficient when paired with audio-only feedback.
  • Speed-Accuracy Trade-off: Over time, participants navigated significantly faster but veered further from the path centerline, indicating they leveraged multimodal redundancy to move aggressively rather than cautiously.

De-Coupling Time from Navigation Success

The non-significant overall Movement Time (MT) results highlight that BLV users can achieve consistent spatial velocities regardless of the locomotion technique provided. However, speed-based performance metrics can mask underlying usability barriers.

Even though participants maintained their speed in the vibration-only condition, they reported a significantly higher perceived workload and poorer environmental understanding. Evaluating non-visual VR accessibility requires looking beyond just how fast a user completes a route.

Qualitative Findings

Thematic analysis of the semi-structured interviews yielded four key narrative themes:

  • Discrete Disconnection: The rhythmic mismatch of discrete teleportation severed the continuous sensory feedback loop, forcing a disruptive "stop-and-scan" bottleneck.
  • Vibration Translation Tax: Participants experienced high cognitive friction translating abstract vest vibrations into spatial movements, whereas audio cues felt ecologically grounded.
  • Thumbstick Friction: The 360-degree vector of the analogue joystick made maintaining a forward vector difficult for non-gamers, who often preferred the 1:1 somatic mapping of arm-swinging.
  • Strategy Changes Due to Redundancy: Participants actively adapted to accept a larger margin of error when provided with combined feedback, using it as a fail-safe to move with momentum.

Conclusion

Key Takeaways:

  • No One-Size-Fits-All: There is no singular optimal locomotion technique for non-visual accessibility. Developers must prioritise customisation over mandating a single method.
  • Mechanical Alignment is Crucial: Continuous movement techniques (sliding joystick) require ecologically grounded auditory channels to prevent spatial drift. Discrete locomotion (teleportation) benefits from proximity-based vibrations to balance discontinuous jumps.
  • Multimodality Empowers Users: Redundant sensory modalities act as a cognitive fail-safe. Allowing users to toggle overlapping non-visual channels ensures navigation strategies can scale with familiarity and application context.
  • Vibration Translation Tax: Vibration-only feedback induced the highest cognitive load and lowest ease-of-use ratings. It should be treated as a powerful supplementary channel rather than an isolated solution.

Reflections

This final empirical study successfully ties together the findings from my entire PhD journey. By observing the direct clash between locomotion mechanics and sensory modalities, it became incredibly clear that accessibility isn't just about adding a feature—it's about ensuring that feature harmonises with the core mechanics of the application.

Seeing participants evolve their strategies over the session—moving from cautious, straight-line walkers to confident, momentum-driven navigators—was incredibly rewarding. It reinforced my belief that BLV users don't just want to "get by" in VR; they want to explore, move fast, and experience the environment with agency.

The lessons learned here about multimodal redundancy and mechanical alignment will form the cornerstone of my final thesis recommendations, establishing a framework for how developers can create truly inclusive spatial computing experiences.
