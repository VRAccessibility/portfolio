Haptic-Only Spatial Target Selection

Empirical Study with 32 Blind Users

Project Overview

Virtual reality (VR) mixed method study with 32 blind and visually impaired (BVI) participants, exploring fundamental movement characteristics in a non-visual spatial VR interface.

Problem

The absence of accommodations and standardised non-visual guidelines has resulted in widespread exclusion from VR for BVI users. Prior VR target acquisition work has explored haptic feedback in combination with visuals or audio, yet its effectiveness on its own has not been thoroughly examined.

Key Finding

(1) The first empirical performance data for a haptics-only VR target acquisition task for BVI users, (2) actionable design recommendations for accessible tactile VR interfaces, and (3) evidence that existing linear predictive models like Fitts' Law inadequately capture non-visual spatial interactions.

Problem Definition

Haptic cues may prove particularly valuable for enhancing access to VR for BVI users, leveraging the tactile sense preferred for spatial sensory substitution. Evaluating fundamental tasks, such as pointing, informs understanding of more complex high-level interactions like spatial orientation and wayfinding.

Although Fitts' Law is suited to evaluating spatial selection tasks for BVI users, current VR implementations of Fitts' Law are predominantly visual-only, underscoring the urgent need to explore non-visual VR. This study addresses this gap by investigating a non-visual VR Fitts' target acquisition task with 32 BVI participants. The Fitts' Law reciprocal task design explores responses to body-based proximity attractive vibrotactile directional cues towards lateral and depth-based targets in peripersonal space.

The literature demonstrates the utility of haptic feedback for guiding BVI users interacting with 2D interfaces and for augmenting the performance of sighted users in spatial target selection tasks. However, a significant gap exists in bridging these domains and translating these principles to fully non-visual VR interactions, largely excluding BVI users from the embodied spatial interactions that define modern VR.

While haptic cues often supplement visual information or provide alerts for obstacle avoidance in VR, their use as the primary and sole modality for spatial target selection by BVI users is a critical, unexplored area. Although prior work suggests single modality cues may be preferred and BVI users express a desire for more haptic information in VR, there is a lack of empirical data on how haptic-only cues perform in target selection scenarios. Consequently, detailed performance metrics that could quantify the effectiveness of haptic cues and model the behaviour of BVI users interacting with targets in VR are absent.

We asked:

  1. To what extent does Fitts' Law model haptic-only VR target acquisition movements for BVI users?
  2. How does performance differ between axes?
  3. How does haptic feedback type influence performance, preference, and usability?

The mixed methods approach, combining standardised performance data analysis and semi-structured interview thematic analysis, provides actionable recommendations for the design of accessible VR interfaces for BVI users.

Methodology

Participants used a single controller in their preferred hand for target selection with a trigger press, with selection errors permissible and no feedback provided to convey accuracy. Haptic cues from the bHaptics TactSuit x40 vest informed participants on target direction and distance, with a single pair of haptic targets along either the lateral or depth axis following a modified ISO 9241-411:2012 (Annex B.6.2.1) reciprocal tapping task design.

Testing occurred from October 2024 to January 2025 with 32 unpaid participants (20 males, 12 females, 0 non-binary/other), aged 24-84 (M = 49.75 ± 16.12), recruited from across the UK to ensure evaluations could be conducted in locations and according to schedules suitable for the BVI participants.

A 2x2 within-subject repeated-measures design explored movement direction (lateral vs. depth) and haptic growth modulation (quadratic vs. pulse) with six commonly used indices of difficulty. Participants alternated between targets appearing along the movement axis. Instructions followed standard Fitts' Law procedures, with continuous movement between targets as quickly and accurately as possible.

In summary, the experimental design was as follows:

  • Total blocks per participant: 4 (one for each direction × feedback method combination)
  • Total trials per block: 108 (6 IDs × 9 trials × 2 repetitions)
  • Total trials per participant: 432 (4 blocks × 108 trials)
  • Total dataset: 13,924 trials (432 trials × 32 participants)

Results

Key significant results include:

  • Faster lateral movements and more errors than depth
  • Higher (effective) throughput laterally than depth
  • Reasonable Fitts' Law fit for movement time, poor fit for errors
  • Smaller and closer targets yielded more errors
  • No learning effects
  • Minimal demographic differences
  • Higher workload and exertion for depth movements

Users systematically overshot targets, particularly laterally and for short distances, suggesting more explicit braking cues are needed.

There was a strong mismatch between perceived accuracy and statistical results, with users believing they performed better laterally than in depth, whilst the results showed the opposite.

Qualitative summary:

  • Initial Challenges and Familiarity: Participants initially faced challenges that diminished as familiarity increased
  • Sense of Achievement and Fun: Participants frequently reported enjoyment and a sense of achievement, reinforcing the motivational potential of haptic cues
  • Desire for Multimodal Feedback: Participants expressed a preference for adding audio feedback
  • Customisation Importance: Varied and sometimes opposing perspectives underscore the complexity of haptic instructions

Conclusion

Key Takeaways:

  • Need for Extended Models - unique for BVI spatial movements
  • Speed-Accuracy Trade-off Across Axes - faster but less accurate lateral movements
  • Quadratic and Pulse Modulation Show Equivalent Results - users adapted equally well
  • Mismatch in Accuracy and Confidence - more explicit instructions are needed
  • Small Target Distances Led to More Errors - fundamentally different to visual behaviour
  • Minimal Learning Effect - BVI users can learn haptic instructions quickly in VR
  • Limited Demographic Differences - highlighting importance of personalisation
  • Participant Enthusiasm Indicates Benefits - suitability for fast exergame instructions
  • Testing Beyond Reciprocal Tasks - full-body tracking capabilities exploration essential
  • Alternative Designs of Haptic Targets - more explicit cues during deceleration needed
  • Haptic Feedback Alone May Not Precisely Guide Movement - haptics appear suited to rapid initial movements, but accuracy challenges, particularly at short distances, highlight how they may not be suitable for all VR inputs
